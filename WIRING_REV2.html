00 How to read this

This is an interconnect diagram, not a circuit-analysis schematic. Every device is a labelled block with its real silkscreen pin names on the edges, because the silkscreen is what you read with a wire in your hand. Only the parts you actually fit as discrete components — the fuse, the switches, the four resistors — are drawn as symbols.

Sheets 1–3 are grouped by subsystem: power, the motor chain, the capture path. Sheet 4 is that same wiring seen from the header — pin 1 at the bottom, laid out the way the connector sits in front of you. Use 1–3 to understand the rig, and 4 to wire it.

Two conventions A filled dot where wires meet is a connection. Wires that cross without a dot are not connected. Every ground symbol ⏚ is the same single net — see the star-point note under Figure 1.

01 Power distribution

The star point Every ⏚ on all three sheets is one net. Land them all at the battery negative terminal — that is the star point. Nine conductors arrive there: BT1(–), U3 IN–, U3 OUT–, U6 IN–, U6 OUT–, U4 GND (power side), U7 J2 pin 2, and the Pi's ground, which reaches the star through U3 OUT–. The Pi's ground and the driver's ground must be common or STEP and DIR have no return path — that is what the red note on Rev 1.1 was about.
Three things to settle before this is energised
  • The XL6009 is a step-up converter. A plain XL6009 module cannot make 5 V from a 12 V input — it can only boost. If U3 is a genuine boost-only module it will pass roughly the input voltage straight through to the Pi's 5 V rail and destroy it. Measure U3's output on the bench, with no Pi connected, and confirm it holds 5.1 V from a 12 V input. If it does not, U3 must be replaced with a buck or buck-boost module.
  • Feeding the Pi through JP1 pins 2 and 4 bypasses its input protection. The USB-C jack has a polyfuse and an e-fuse behind it; the header pins have neither. The supply must be accurate (5.1 V) and current-limited, and its polarity is unforgiving.
  • The VLP-16 wants 12 V nominal. S2 hands it raw battery volts. On a 24 V pack that is outside the sensor's input range — check the pack's actual voltage before S2 is closed, or put a regulator in that leg.

02 Motor chain — Pi header to driver to motor

R_PU is the gating item — nothing turns until it is fitted Every Pi GPIO floats as an input for the ~30 s the Pi takes to boot, and ENABLE is active-low. Without the pull-up the driver can sit energised through the whole of boot with nothing in control of it. Two details decide whether the resistor actually does its job:
  • It goes on the driver side of R_EN — at the ENABLE pin itself, as drawn. Put it on the Pi side and it stops holding ENABLE high the moment the Pi is unplugged.
  • It pulls up to the driver's own VCC, not the Pi's 3V3. VCC is an output from the driver's on-board regulator, selected by the 3/5V APWR jumper. Set that jumper to 3.3 V and measure VCC before wiring anything to it. Then ENABLE is held disabled whenever the driver has power, whether the Pi is running, booting or absent — and no 5 V can ever reach GPIO13.
Note: PROJECT_CONTEXT.md says pull up to +3V3 and MICROVIEW_REMOVAL.md says pull up to the driver's VCC. They disagree. VCC at 3.3 V is the version that also covers "Pi off, driver on", so that is what is drawn here.
Coil pairing is the one motor mistake that matters A1/A2 must be the two ends of the same coil, and B1/B2 the two ends of the other. Get a wire from coil A onto a B output and the motor will buzz, heat and not turn. Which way round the two ends of a coil go only reverses the direction — and direction is a software setting (TLSPIE_DIR_FORWARD=0), so do not chase it with a soldering iron.

Find the pairs with a multimeter on continuity, motor disconnected: the two wires of one coil read a few ohms to each other and open circuit to the other pair.

Vin goes to 3V3 — header pin 1, not pin 2 or 4 On this breakout the I²C pull-up resistors reference Vin, so whatever you feed Vin is what appears on SDA and SCL when the bus is idle. Put 5 V on Vin and the board pulls GPIO2 and GPIO3 to 5 V — and the Pi's GPIOs are not 5 V tolerant. The DS3231 runs happily from 2.3 V and draws microamps, so there is no reason to use 5 V here even if it were safe.

Check it before you trust it: power the board from pin 1 with the SDA and SCL wires not yet fitted, and measure SDA to GND. It must read about 3.3 V, not 5.

Four things about this board specifically
  • The pin order is not the order the header hands the wires over in. The breakout runs Vin, GND, SCL, SDA — the Pi's four pins arrive as 3V3, SDA, SCL, GND. Two of the four wires cross. That is drawn honestly on Sheet 4 rather than tidied away.
  • SDA and SCL are easy to swap and the symptom is silence, not damage. i2cdetect simply finds nothing. Check the pair before blaming the board.
  • No battery-charging circuit. That is the difference between this board and the cheap ZS-042 clones, which do have one and slowly cook a non-rechargeable cell. Here a plain coin cell is exactly right — fit it + side up.
  • Address 0x68 is fixed and nothing else in the rig is on I²C, so there is no conflict to design around.
Wiring it is half the job — the Pi ignores it until you say so Raspberry Pi OS keeps time with fake-hwclock, which just restores the last known time at boot. The RTC only takes over once the kernel driver is bound:

This matters more here than on a desk machine. The rig runs off a phone hotspot with no guaranteed internet, and every scan's sidecar and filename is timestamped — without the RTC a cold boot in the field dates your captures from whenever the Pi was last switched off.

04 JP1 — the header as you hold it

Drawn in the orientation of your pinout card: pin 1 at the bottom, odd numbers up the right column, even numbers up the left, the header running down the board’s left edge. Eleven pins carry wires — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 33, 35, 37 and 39. One is damaged. The other twenty-eight are free, and stay free.

Which end is pin 1 Pin 1 is the corner of the header nearest the USB-C power and micro-HDMI edge of the board. In this orientation that puts it at the bottom, 3V3 on the right of the pair and 5 V on the left. Count up in twos from there and the header cannot end up reversed — which is the mistake that destroyed the MicroView.

Pin 13 · GPIO27 Confirmed damaged by gpio_selftest.py on 2026-08-09 — it drives both rails but its internal pull-up no longer holds it high as an input, consistent with the 12 V incident. It was the old RECORDSTOP line. Nothing in Rev 2.0 uses it. Never put an input there. GPIO14 and GPIO15, the two pins that ran to the destroyed harness, both pass.

Ground pins are electrically identical, so pins 6, 9 and 39 were chosen for tidiness rather than function: 6 sits next to the fan's 5 V on pin 4, 9 next to the I²C pair, and 39 next to the three motor signals at the far end. Keep the motor's return on 39 — it keeps the driver's ground wire away from the I²C pair.

Keep S1 (Main) and S2 (Lidar) are power switches, not buttons. S1 is the rig's emergency stop: it cuts both the Pi's converter and the motor rail, so the coils de-energise and STEP stops toggling — and unlike a switch in series with ENABLE, a crashed Pi with the DMA engine still clocking pulses cannot defeat it. Check S1's DC rating before trusting it: it breaks a DC inductive load, DC arcs do not self-extinguish the way AC ones do, and an under-rated switch slowly welds its contacts. A welded E-stop looks fine right up until it is needed.

07 Bring-up order

No motor has ever turned on this rig. Build the wiring in this order and each step is provable before the next one can hurt anything.

  1. Header disconnected — run ./gpio_selftest.py. Expect 25 of 26 pins healthy and GPIO27 output-only.
  2. Converters on the bench, no loads. Confirm U3 holds 5.1 V and U6 holds 12 V from the real pack. This is where a boost-only XL6009 shows itself.
  3. Ground first. Star-point every return, then check continuity from the Pi's pin 39 to the battery negative before any supply is connected.
  4. Power the Pi only. Panel reachable at http://tlspie.local:8080/, nothing else wired.
  5. Fit R_PU, then the three series resistors, then the signal wires. With the driver powered and the Pi off, measure ENABLE — it must sit at VCC, not near zero.
  6. Motor uncoupled from the head. ./tls_scan.py --scan scan3 --no-record, then press Stop mid-move and confirm the motor halts.
  7. Command 90° and measure it with the shaft marked. This is the outstanding question: 640,000 steps/rev is measured and solid, but whether that comes from a 100:1 reduction or a 0.45°/step motor is still unsettled.
  8. Full scan with capture. Watch for lost steps while tcpdump runs — DMA and capture contend for memory bandwidth, and that is the one real open performance question.
Before the first move, verify on the board itself
  • MS1/MS2/MS3 open = 1/16 microstep on a Big Easy Driver, via its on-board pull-ups. The measured 640,000 steps/rev assumes 1/16 — if a jumper has been moved, the geometry constant is wrong.
  • RST and SLP are tied together on the board on a genuine Big Easy Driver, so both may be left open. Put a meter across them and confirm continuity. If they are not tied, jumper RST to SLP or the driver never leaves reset and the motor will simply never move.
  • Set the current limit on the ADJ PWR pot before the motor sees load.
  • Confirm the Pi really is 192.168.1.100 against the VLP-16's own configuration. That number was never verified against the sensor.
